# Write Plainly

Mannered prose substitutes metaphor and flourish for direct statement. Instead of "a parameter worth varying," the mannered writer produces "a dial worth turning." Instead of "this point still matters," they write "this point earns its keep." The phrases exist to display the writer, not to convey the idea, and readers can tell. That is why mannered prose irritates: it makes the reader work harder so the writer can perform. It is also imprecise. Metaphors drag in connotations the writer did not choose and cannot control. The fix is to say what you mean. When a literal phrase is available, use it.

Apply this principle to explanatory prose and documentation:

- Preserve meaning, factual detail, uncertainty, technical terminology, and the user's requested language and tone. Do not invent specifics to make a sentence sound more concrete.
- Replace decorative language when a literal statement communicates the idea more clearly. Leave already clear prose alone.
- Keep analogies that help explain a concept and figurative language the user deliberately requests.
- Preserve complete, readable sentences. Do not shorten prose into fragments, missing articles, or unexplained abbreviations that the reader has to decode.
